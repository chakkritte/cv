Why Tense Matters

In academic writing, verb tense isn't just about grammar; it's a tool that signals context to your reader. The tense you choose tells them whether you are stating an established fact, reporting a past finding, or proposing future work. Using tenses correctly and consistently builds credibility and makes your argument clear.

1. Introduction

Your introduction sets the stage. You'll move from general background to the specific problem your paper addresses.

This chart shows a typical mix. Use the Present Tense to state the problem or general facts. Use the Present Perfect to highlight gaps in existing research ("Researchers *have focused* on X, but Y *has not been* studied").

2. Literature Review

Here, you review previous work. The tense you use depends on what you're describing.

Use the Past Tense to cite specific, completed studies (e.g., "Smith (2020) *found*..."). Use the Present Tense to discuss established theories or knowledge (e.g., "This theory *states*...").

3. Methods

This section is a straightforward report of what you *did* to conduct your research.

The rule is simple: the Past Tense is dominant. You are describing actions you completed in the past (e.g., "We *collected* data..." or "The sample *was selected*...").

4. Results

Like the Methods section, the Results section reports on what you *found* from your experiments.

The Past Tense is used to present your findings (e.g., "The data *showed* a correlation..."). You may use the Present Tense only to refer to figures or tables (e.g., "Table 1 *shows*...").

5. Discussion

This is where you interpret your findings and connect them back to the wider field. This requires a mix of tenses.

This chart shows the balance. Use the Present Tense to interpret your results and state implications ("This *suggests*..."). Use the Past Tense to compare your findings to specific past studies ("Our results *differed* from Smith (2020)...").

6. Conclusion

Your conclusion summarizes the paper's main points and highlights its significance.

The Present Tense is strong here, used to state the overall conclusions and implications. You'll use the Past Tense to briefly restate your key findings, and may use the Future Tense for recommendations.

Tense Flow Through Your Paper

Think of your paper as a journey. You start with the present context, move into the past to describe your work, and return to the present to explain what it all means.
